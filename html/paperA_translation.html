Fitness landscapes and epigenetic landscapes are the same algorithm

with different representational capacity
AbstractProtein fitness landscapes and Waddington-style epigenetic landscapes are usually treated as separate objects, studied by separate communities. We show that two representative models—a variational-autoencoder latent-space model of protein fitness and a Hopfield-network attractor model of disease progression—instantiate the same six-role computational template: a state, a learned representation, a scalar field over that representation, a transition operator, a trajectory rule, and a feedback term. We distinguish three levels of equivalence (shared vocabulary, shared algorithmic role structure, shared mathematical object) and show that the two models are equivalent at the second level but not the third: the fitness surface is a nonparametric Gaussian process, whereas the attractor energy is a fixed quadratic form. A synthetic double-well benchmark makes the gap quantitative—the Gaussian-process surface recovers the ground-truth landscape (Spearman $\rho=1.00$, all four basins), while the quadratic energy does not ($\rho=0.34$, zero interior minima). The gap identifies a concrete unification target: a learned scalar potential equipped with an explicit transition operator, fit jointly to longitudinal fitness and expression-trajectory data. The two papers share no references.

Introduction

The metaphor of a landscape is common to two research communities that rarely cite one another. In molecular evolution, a fitness landscape assigns a scalar fitness to each genotype, and adaptation is ascent on that surface. In systems biology, Waddington's epigenetic landscape (Waddington 2014) pictures a cell rolling into one of several valleys, formalised as attractors of a gene-regulatory network (Huang 2005). Both communities have since replaced the metaphor with fitted models — learned fitness surfaces on one side (Riesselman 2018, Rives 2021), quantified potential landscapes on the other (Wang 2011, Huang 2009).

Whether these are the same object, or merely share a picture, is usually left unexamined. We examine it for a specific pair of papers: Ding 2019, who learn a protein fitness landscape in a variational-autoencoder latent space, and Fard 2017, who model disease progression as motion on a Hopfield attractor landscape. The two share no references. We show that they fill the same six-role computational template, and that the correspondence holds at the level of algorithmic role but breaks at the level of mathematical object — a distinction we make quantitative on a synthetic benchmark, and which specifies what a unified model must contain.

Two models

Model A: a latent-space model of protein fitness

Ding 2019 learn a latent representation of a protein family and fit a scalar fitness surface over it. A sequence $x$ (one-hot over the columns of a multiple sequence alignment) is encoded by a variational autoencoder (Kingma 2013) with encoder $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ and decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$, trained by maximising the evidence lower bound
\[\mathrm{ELBO}(\theta,\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\right] -\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z)\right), \qquad p_\theta(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I).\]
The first term is reconstruction from the latent code; the second regularises the posterior toward the prior. Writing $z=\phi(x)$ for the (two-dimensional) encoding, experimental fitness or stability labels are then regressed onto $z$ by Gaussian-process regression (Rasmussen 2005) with a radial-basis kernel,
\[J(x) \;=\; f\!\left(\phi(x)\right), \qquad f \sim \mathcal{GP}\!\left(m(\cdot),\,k_{\mathrm{RBF}}(\cdot,\cdot)\right).\]
This yields a continuous scalar field over the latent space. Mutations displace $x$ and therefore $z$; an adaptive walk is ascent on $J$.

Model B: an attractor-landscape model of disease progression

Fard 2017, explicitly invoking Waddington's epigenetic landscape (Waddington 2014) and the attractor view of cell fate (Huang 2005, Huang 2009), represent a cell by a gene-expression pattern $P$ and build a Hopfield network (Hopfield 1982) whose symmetric weight matrix is the gene–gene correlation,
\[W_{ij} = \mathrm{PCC}(i,j), \qquad W_{ii}=0 .\]
The dynamics are the discrete Hopfield update
\[P(t+1) = \mathrm{sgn}\!\left(P(t)\,W\right),\]
and the associated Lyapunov energy, which decreases monotonically under Eq. 4, is
\[E[P] = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,P\,W\,P^{\top}.\]
Attractors are local minima of Eq. 5 and correspond to normal and disease cell states; disease progression is the trajectory converging to an attractor. The landscape is visualised by interpolating $E$ over the first two principal components of the expression data.

A shared formal template

Both models can be written in a common six-slot template. Let $x\in\mathcal{S}$ be the system state, $z=\phi(x)$ a learned or fixed representation, $J$ a scalar field, $T$ a transition operator with control input $u$, $\pi$ a trajectory rule, and $F$ a feedback term:

\[\begin{aligned}x_t &\in \mathcal{S}, & z &= \phi(x), & J&:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}, \\ x_{t+1} &= T(x_t,u_t), & \pi &= \text{trajectory rule on } J, & F &= \text{feedback / selection}.\end{aligned}\]

Three levels of equivalence

Claims that two models are “the same” conflate three distinct assertions, which we separate:

Level 1 — shared vocabulary. Both use the words landscape, state, and trajectory. This holds trivially and carries no content: the terms are metaphors in wide circulation.

Level 2 — shared algorithmic role structure. Both fit a scalar field over a representation of state and interpret dynamics as motion on that field toward extrema that are the biologically meaningful configurations. Each of the six roles in Eq. 6–Eq. 7 is occupied on both sides. This holds, and it is the substantive claim of this paper.

Level 3 — same mathematical object. This fails. The fitness surface Eq. 2 is a Gaussian process: a nonparametric prior over functions that can represent an arbitrary smooth multi-well surface. The attractor energy Eq. 5 is a fixed quadratic form determined entirely by the single matrix $W$. A quadratic form has one stationary structure — its curvature is constant and set by the eigenvalues of $W$ — so it cannot represent two separated minima with a barrier between them without additional machinery. The two are the same type of construct (a scalar potential over a state representation) but not the same object.

A synthetic benchmark separates Level 2 from Level 3

To make the Level-3 distinction quantitative rather than rhetorical, we constructed a ground-truth landscape with a known number of attractors and fit both formalisms to data drawn from it. The generating potential is a coupled double well on a two-dimensional state $s=(s_1,s_2)$,

\[U(s) = (s_1^2-1)^2 + (s_2^2-1)^2 - 0.6\,s_1 s_2 ,\]
which has two dominant basins (a “normal” basin near $(-1,-1)$ and a “disease” basin near $(+1,+1)$). We drew $150$ samples near each basin for the Hopfield route, and $220$ scattered labelled points for the Gaussian-process route, and fit each model by its own paper's procedure: for Model A, GP regression of the scalar label on the state; for Model B, the correlation weight matrix Eq. 3 and energy Eq. 5.

Agreement with the ground truth was measured by Spearman rank correlation over a $70\times70$ grid, and basin structure by counting interior local minima. The Gaussian-process surface reproduced the generating potential exactly in rank order ($\rho=1.00$) and recovered all four local minima. The Hopfield energy achieved $\rho=0.34$ and recovered zero interior minima: the quadratic form collapses the double well into a single saddle (Figure~Figure 2). The failure is structural, not a matter of sample size or tuning.

Negative controls

A correspondence procedure is only informative if it also rejects. We therefore applied the same six-role mapping to three false-friend pairs drawn from the same corpus: cross-literature pairs with high semantic similarity but low similarity in computational role. In each case the dominant role differs between the two papers, and the mapping rejects the pair (Figure~Figure 3):

  • objective versus state (semantic similarity $0.76$, role similarity $0.24$): a generative model of single-cell time series against a cell-fate classifier — one fits a probability flow, the other locates a state.
  • state versus representation (semantic $0.63$, role $0.00$): sequence-to-expression prediction against transfer-entropy network reconstruction — one is a readout, the other builds a network.
  • representation versus search (semantic $0.65$, role $0.23$): static binding-affinity prediction against learning pathway dynamics — one is static, the other dynamical.
All three have higher surface similarity than the accepted pair, and all three are rejected. The procedure is therefore not matching on vocabulary.

Failure modes of the correspondence

  1. Equilibrium versus trajectory. Model A fits a static surface to a population assumed at or near equilibrium; Model B is a dynamical system with an explicit time update but no learned surface shape. Neither model does both. The learning sits on opposite sides: Model A learns the representation and fixes the objective family; Model B fixes the representation and reads the objective off it.
  2. Representational capacity. As shown above, GP versus quadratic form. Any translation that treats the two potentials as interchangeable will over-claim.
  3. Meaning of the latent coordinate. Model A's $z$ is learned by the VAE; Model B's coordinates are a fixed principal-component basis used only for visualisation, with the dynamics running in the full state space. Equating the two axes is a category error.
  4. Sign convention. Fitness is maximised, energy minimised. The flip is trivial but must be stated: on our synthetic benchmark, comparing the two fields without aligning sign yields a spurious negative correlation ($\rho=-0.34$, versus $+0.34$ once aligned).

What the gap specifies

The Level-3 gap is not a defect of the correspondence; it is its most useful output, because it specifies what a unified model must contain. Model A supplies a learned scalar field of arbitrary shape but no dynamics; Model B supplies explicit dynamics but a scalar field of fixed, inadequate capacity. The unification is therefore a single object with both properties: a learned potential $J_\psi$ (Gaussian process, normalising flow, or neural potential) equipped with an explicit transition operator,

\[x_{t+1} = T\!\left(x_t,u_t\right) = x_t - \eta \,\nabla J_\psi(x_t) + \sigma\,\xi_t ,\]
with $u_t$ the environmental or perturbation input, $\xi_t$ noise, and $J_\psi$ fit jointly to fitness-like labels and to observed state trajectories. Because $J_\psi$ is allowed to depend on time or on $u$, the resulting object is a seascape rather than a landscape in the sense of Mustonen 2009, and it inherits the attractor semantics of Wang 2011. The experiment this implies is concrete: fit Eq. 9 to a system for which both longitudinal deep-mutational-scanning data and expression-state trajectories exist, and test whether the inferred minima of $J_\psi$ coincide with the attractors recovered by the Hopfield route.

Discussion

The result is deliberately narrow: two papers, one correspondence, one benchmark. Its value is that each claim is checkable. The broader implication — that pairs of this kind can be found systematically, by measuring computational-role similarity separately from semantic similarity and citation overlap — is the subject of separate work; the pair analysed here was identified that way, and the negative controls in Figure~Figure 3 are drawn from the same procedure.

We emphasise a limitation. The benchmark in Figure~Figure 2 is synthetic: it establishes that the two formalisms differ in representational capacity, not that this difference matters for any particular biological system. The decisive experiment is the one implied by Eq. 9, on a system with both longitudinal fitness measurements and expression-state trajectories.
