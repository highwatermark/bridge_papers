**Mustonen & Lässig 2009** is a review of the same framework. In the regime $\mu N\ll1$ it treats the state as a sequence and dynamics as Kimura–Ohta substitution rates $u_{a\to b}=\mu_{a\to b}f(\Delta F,N)$. Its equilibrium is $Q_{eq}\propto Q^0_{eq}e^{2NF}$, and time dependence comes from a two-parameter "minimal seascape model (strength and rate of selection changes)". ### B. The five pre-set questions, answered from the text

1. **State.** The seascape state is a **population** (frequency vector; 2010) or a **lineage's sequence** (2009, substitution regime), not an individual. Cislo's state is a single cell. The ensemble distributions therefore correspond as *cells in a culture* ↔ *replicate populations or lineages*. They are not cell ↔ genotype.
2. **Scalar field.** Yes. $F(x,t)$ is defined over the same state the dynamics act on.
3. **Dynamics.** Yes. Both are drift–diffusion (Fokker–Planck) equations with drift from a scalar-field gradient passed through a metric. The noise enters differently. In Mustonen–Lässig the same state-dependent matrix $g(x)$ sets both the mobility and the noise, and $1/N$ plays the role of temperature. In Cislo the drift uses $g^{-1}$, the noise is isotropic with scale $D$, and the fitting pipeline assumes a **spatially uniform** $g=gI$.
4. **Exogenous or feedback.** In both, the time dependence is an **externally imposed protocol**: ecology in one, morphogen dose in the other. Neither model has the state reshape the landscape. Mustonen–Lässig does allow **non-gradient** selection (e.g. rock-paper-scissors), which Cislo's Morse–Smale, gradient-like assumption rules out.
5. **Inference.** Cislo **fits** $U$ to time-resolved snapshot distributions by relative entropy. Mustonen–Lässig **does not fit** a seascape. It derives a theorem and checks it against phage, bacterial and *Drosophila* data. The 2010 paper does note that its Eq. 2 "has been used to infer scaled fitness landscapes $NF(x)$ from histograms" of genomic data (its refs 28, 29). That is the same log-density step as Cislo's $U_B=-\log p_B$ and $U_{\text{eff}}=-gD\log p_{eq}$.

### C. Term-by-term correspondence

Both equilibria factor into a base (neutral) part and a directed part:

$$\text{Mustonen–Lässig: } P_{eq}(x)=P_0(x)\,e^{NF(x)}\;\Rightarrow\;-\log P_{eq}=\underbrace{-\log P_0}_{\text{mutation–drift}}\;\underbrace{-\,NF}_{\text{selection}}$$
$$\text{Cislo: } U_D=\underbrace{U_B=-\log p_B}_{\text{undirected diffusion}}+\underbrace{U_I}_{\text{fitted directed part}}$$

Proposed map: $U_B\leftrightarrow-\log P_0$, $U_I\leftrightarrow-NF$, $D\leftrightarrow1/N$, $\theta(t)\leftrightarrow$ the seascape's time dependence.

The map is *structural*. It follows from both being drift–diffusion processes with a Boltzmann-type equilibrium. Neither paper states it.

### D. Verdict — three levels of equivalence

- **Level 1 (vocabulary):** yes.
- **Level 2 (role structure):** **yes, on 5 of 7 slots**. This is the strongest adjudicated case so far. The shared absence of learned representation and of feedback is part of the match.
- **Level 3 (same mathematical object):** **same family, not the same object as each paper uses it.** Both are Fokker–Planck dynamics under an externally time-dependent scalar potential with a base-times-exponential equilibrium. They coincide exactly only in the special case of a gradient field with a **uniform metric and isotropic noise**. They diverge in three ways:
  - (i) Wright–Fisher mobility and noise $g(x)$ depend on the state and are tied together, while Cislo's pipeline fixes $g$ uniform.
  - (ii) Mustonen–Lässig allows non-gradient drift, which Cislo excludes.
  - (iii) The state units differ (cell vs population).

  Unlike Ding–Fard, neither side is *unable* to represent the other's landscape. The gap is in the noise and metric term and in the unit of state, not in capacity.

### E. Failure modes

- **Unit mismatch.** A correspondence at the level of cells-in-a-culture ↔ populations-in-replicates is defensible. Cell ↔ genotype is not.
- **Misspecified noise.** Running Cislo's pipeline on evolutionary data with uniform $g$ would mis-model Wright–Fisher noise, which vanishes at fixation boundaries. Any transfer has to carry $g(x)$ over.
- **Non-gradient selection.** Frequency-dependent cycles have no potential. Cislo's method would force one.
- **Theory vs inference.** The seascape side has a theorem but no fitted $F(x,t)$. The bridge is therefore a *methods transfer* (Cislo's inference applied to evolution time series), plus a *theory transfer* (a fitness-flux / fluctuation theorem applied to development). It is not a claim that the two literatures already compute the same thing.

### F. What the bridge suggests, and the test (not run)

**Experiment neither field has.** Two transfers:
- **Fitness side:** infer a time-dependent seascape from replicate evolution-experiment time series with Cislo's snapshot-fitting pipeline, extended to state-dependent $g(x)$.
- **Development side:** compute a *fitness-flux analogue* (cumulative $\sum\Delta x\cdot\nabla U$) along morphogen protocols. Test whether Mustonen–Lässig's fluctuation identity $\langle e^{-N\Phi+\Delta H}\rangle=1$ has a counterpart that quantifies irreversibility after transient morphogen exposure. Cislo reports that irreversibility qualitatively.

**Synthetic test.**
- **Setup:** simulate Wright–Fisher populations (1–2 loci, many replicates) under a known switching seascape, and fit the snapshots two ways: (a) uniform $g$, as in Cislo's pipeline; (b) Wright–Fisher $g(x)$.
- **Prediction, fixed now:** (b) recovers the seascape's optima and the post-switch transient; (a) biases the potential near the boundaries.
- **Negative control:** a rock-paper-scissors (non-gradient) seascape should fail under both.
